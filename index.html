The Lantern Philosophy Version: 1.7

The Lantern Philosophy is a living, evolving framework that begins with a simple belief: our minds are the lanterns we carry into the darkness. We do not seek to illuminate the world with final answers, but with reason, curiosity, and better questions. Through freedom, experience, creativity, and continuous discovery, we seek a freer, more conscious, and more livable world while remembering that no idea, including our own, should ever be beyond question.

The Five Lights

  1. 1. Begin with freedom.

  2. 2. Follow the reasons.

  3. 3. Live deliberately.

  4. 4. Let life flourish.

  5. 5. Never stop becoming.

Manifesto

The way we understand the world shapes the way we live in it. How we seek knowledge influences how we make decisions; how we make decisions shapes how we treat others; and how we live shapes the meaning we create.

Begin with freedom. We believe that every person has a natural desire to grow, develop, pursue what they value, and shape their lives, and we seek to create the freedom and space for them to do so. Each person has the final say over their own life, body, gender, sexual orientation, beliefs, thoughts, and identity. We trust people before treating them as potential wrongdoers, and favor voluntary cooperation and local self-governance over imposed authority. We defend freedom of belief and neither affirm nor deny supernatural realities. We remain open to questioning what we believe we know and to revising our understanding when stronger reasons emerge.

Follow the reasons. We examine ideas without prejudice, regardless of where they come from. We break them down, examine their parts through evidence and reason, question their assumptions, compare them with experience and alternative explanations, and bring them together again to understand the whole. We move between the particular and the general, discuss alternatives, and seek common ground or the strongest conclusion available. Any conclusion may be questioned again and changed when better reasons emerge. The same principle applies to collective decisions: we favor voluntary cooperation, open participation, and consensus where possible, while allowing decisions and procedures themselves to be questioned and revised.

Live deliberately. We do not want to live by habit, expectation, or inherited answers alone. To live deliberately is to pay attention to the life we are actually living, to choose where our time and attention go, and to take responsibility for the direction we give them. We may pause, rest, explore, create, change direction, or simply experience the world without needing to turn every moment into a means toward something else. A deliberate life is not a perfectly planned life, but a life lived with awareness rather than by default.

Let life flourish. We value life and the living world. We do not see nature merely as a resource for human use. We want the conditions of life to allow people, communities, and the wider living world to thrive. Friendship, solidarity, sharing, socializing, and creating together are part of this flourishing. A free individual does not have to be a solitary individual.

Never stop becoming. We do not consider ourselves, our knowledge, or our philosophy finished. We remain willing to learn from experience, encounter unfamiliar ideas, discover our own errors, and change when there is reason to change. What we believe today is part of an ongoing process, not a final definition of who we are. To keep becoming is to remain open to what we may yet learn, and to what we may yet become.

Lanterns

A Lantern is not a doctrine, but a disposition: a way of thinking carried into life. It is a mind in motion, guided by reason and free thought, always questioning, always testing itself, and never mistaking a current view for a final one.

To be a Lantern is not to have arrived, but to keep walking. Certainty is not the goal; the willingness to question, learn, and revise is.

A Lantern can shine alone. Together, different lights can reveal more.

How We Decide

We begin by defining the question clearly and separating the problem into its relevant parts. We distinguish what we know from what we assume, gather available evidence, knowledge and experience, and consider both supporting and opposing views. We actively look for weaknesses in our own reasoning and welcome criticism, including perspectives that challenge our preferred conclusions. Some principles begin not as conclusions, but as starting points for how we understand human life and what we seek to protect.

We then consider the possible paths forward. Rather than limiting ourselves to the most obvious choices, we look for alternative approaches and examine their likely consequences, including effects on freedom, well-being, relationships, trust, and the wider living world. When our values come into conflict, we weigh them according to their importance in the situation and the strength of the reasons behind them. A decision supported by more people is not automatically a better one; our shared values and the reasons supporting each choice must also be considered.

We seek the strongest available conclusion rather than certainty. Those affected by a decision should have a meaningful opportunity to understand the alternatives and contribute their views, while evidence, reason, and ethical principles remain part of the judgment. We then choose the best-supported course of action, observe its consequences, and remain willing to reconsider it when experience, evidence, or better alternatives give us reason to do so.

Ethics

Our ethical framework is not merely a list of ethical rules, but a method for determining how ethical thought should be formed. The method itself can also be questioned.

We value human beings and all life, giving greater ethical consideration to beings we have reason to believe are capable of experience, well-being, or pursuing interests of their own. We also value the integrity and continuity of the natural world and its living systems. These values guide our ethical judgments, while the circumstances, reasons, evidence, and consequences help us determine how they should be applied.

Our ethical understanding is not determined by tradition, authority, or personal belief alone. Our moral claims should rest on reasons that can be examined by others and should be capable of changing in the face of evidence and stronger reasons.

We believe freedom should begin with trust, not suspicion. We should not treat people as potential wrongdoers or limit their choices simply because harm is possible. When there is a serious and well-supported risk of harm to another ethically significant being, we first seek voluntary, cooperative, and locally developed ways of addressing it, favoring solutions shaped as closely as possible by the people affected rather than imposed by distant or centralized authority. If intervention becomes necessary, it should be limited to what the situation reasonably requires. We consider the seriousness, scope, duration, and reversibility of the harm, while seeking alternatives that can protect, repair, or support those affected without unnecessarily reducing freedom.

The Purpose of Life

We believe it is still too early to reach firm conclusions about consciousness and the nature of human agency, including free will. The evidence available so far is not sufficient for us to reach a definitive conclusion. We expect our understanding of these questions to develop as neuroscience, cognitive science, and artificial intelligence advance.

We do not know with certainty what, if anything, follows death. Based on our current understanding of consciousness as a process arising from the functioning of the living brain, our strongest working view is that when that process ends, there is no conscious experience afterward, but this conclusion remains open to question and revision.

We also acknowledge that we do not know whether the universe has an ultimate meaning.

Given the limits of human understanding, we remain cautious about judging people for uncertainty about questions we may not yet be able to answer.

We believe that greater understanding of these questions may help us better understand the purpose of life. Given what we currently understand about the brain and consciousness, we do not believe that there is a single predetermined purpose of life for everyone.

A person may discover, create, change, or never define their own purpose with certainty. What matters is not finding a ready-made answer, but experiencing, learning, creating, loving, wondering, forming connections, and freely searching for the path that gives meaning to their own life.

Creativity allows us not only to understand and experience the world, but to give something new back to it. Art is one of the ways we explore existence, express what we experience, and find beauty and meaning in being alive. Beauty may not have a single objective definition, yet our experience of it can still be meaningful.

Through thought, experience, relationships, and creation, we can give shape and meaning to the lives we choose to live.

Applications

The Lantern Philosophy is not a fixed list of political or personal rules. Its principles can lead to practical preferences, but each application remains open to criticism and revision. The following are some directions we currently find especially worth pursuing.

Education and Access to Knowledge.

A community cannot meaningfully improve itself if its members are prevented from learning, regardless of how that community is organized. Education develops the ability to question, understand evidence, participate in decisions, and shape one's own life.

We therefore support broad access to education and knowledge, including freely accessible educational materials wherever possible. Knowledge created for the public should not be unnecessarily kept beyond the reach of the public.

Technology, Work, and Prosperity.

We want technological progress, including artificial intelligence and automation, to expand human freedom and prosperity. As technology allows us to produce more with less human labor, we believe its benefits should be used so that people can spend less of their lives working merely to meet basic needs.

We believe people should have greater freedom to choose work they find meaningful, useful, or enjoyable. Systems that reduce unnecessary economic dependence and give people and communities greater control over their lives can create more space for learning, creating, caring, and pursuing what they value.

Value, Effort, and Creation.

We believe that understanding value requires looking beyond what is immediately visible. Outcomes matter, but they do not tell the whole story. The value of a work, idea, or achievement cannot be measured solely by its outcome. Behind every result are learning, education, experience, failure, thought, time, and physical or mental effort.

We therefore seek value not only in what is achieved, but also in the process that made achievement possible.

Health and Basic Needs.

We believe everyone should have reliable access to the basic conditions needed to live and exercise meaningful freedom, including essential health care, food, shelter, and education. How this responsibility is organized should remain open to experimentation, local decision-making, and revision as communities learn what works best.

Reproduction, Children, and Community.

We believe decisions about whether to continue a pregnancy belong ultimately to the person whose body is carrying it. We therefore believe abortion should remain available throughout pregnancy.

People should remain free to decide whether and when to have children, without coercive population control, while having access to accurate information, education, and family planning so they can understand the consequences of those choices.

We also believe that raising a child is not solely a private responsibility. Until a child can meaningfully direct their own life, their well-being is a shared responsibility of their family and the wider community. Families should remain free to raise their children according to their own values and ways of life, while children should have the care, protection, education, and opportunity to develop into independent people. The wider community should support families in this responsibility without unnecessarily replacing their role.

Food and the Living World.

Human population, consumption, and the way we produce food place real pressures on the living world. These pressures should be considered when making decisions about the future, especially as population and resource demands continue to change. We should also consider the health of the planet and the well-being of future generations.

Food should be considered in the same wider context. We should examine nutrition, animal welfare, environmental impact, resource use, culture, and accessibility together rather than treating one dietary choice as universally correct. Current evidence gives us strong reasons to explore more sustainable and plant-rich food systems, while leaving room for further evidence and individual circumstances.

Euthanasia and Assisted Dying.

We believe a person should have the right to make their own decisions about the end of their life, including choosing euthanasia or assisted dying where continued life is no longer wanted. Such decisions should be voluntary, informed, and carried out through safe and appropriate methods that protect the person from coercion and unnecessary suffering.

Violence and the Use of Force.

We do not support violence, the use of weapons, or the use of force. This includes war, domestic violence, violence against animals and other beings capable of experience or well-being, and all other forms of coercion. We believe in peaceful coexistence.

Intellectual Lineage

Our path is illuminated by many thinkers and traditions: Socrates, Aristotle, René Descartes, John Locke, David Hume, John Stuart Mill, Henry David Thoreau, Charles Darwin, Friedrich Nietzsche, Bertrand Russell, Peter Kropotkin, Jean-Paul Sartre, Albert Camus, Karl Popper, Thomas Kuhn, Daniel Dennett, Peter Singer, Carl Sagan, anarchist thought, humanism, existentialism, agnosticism, pacifism, pragmatism, utilitarian thought, and Bayesian reasoning.

We do not treat any of them as authorities, but as sources of ideas to examine, question, combine, and revise. They offer ways of thinking about reason, freedom, knowledge, ethics, human flourishing, and the consequences of our choices.

Carry the Lantern Forward

The Lantern Philosophy is an attempt to build a framework for thinking, living, and acting toward a better life, guided by what we learn and always open to growth, discovery, and new possibilities.

The path has no fixed end. We carry the light forward by remaining curious, thinking together, and leaving room for what we have yet to discover.

Question. Listen. Doubt. Learn. Experience. Change.
